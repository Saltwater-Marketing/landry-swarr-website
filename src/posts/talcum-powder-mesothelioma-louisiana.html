When most people think about asbestos exposure, they picture shipyards, factories, refineries, or construction sites.

But asbestos exposure allegations have also involved everyday consumer products, including certain talc-containing powders.

A recent New Orleans jury verdict has brought renewed attention to the relationship between talcum powder, asbestos, and mesothelioma.

In Roy v. Johnson & Johnson, an Orleans Parish jury awarded $10 million in total damages to the family of Lynne Roy, who died from mesothelioma.

The jury found that Ms. Roy had significant exposure to asbestos in a Johnson & Johnson product and that the exposure was a cause of her disease. Johnson & Johnson was held responsible for approximately $2 million of the total award, with the remainder attributed to other parties.

The case raises an important question for Louisiana families: What should people understand about talcum powder and possible asbestos exposure?

Are Talc and Asbestos the Same Thing?

No. Talc and asbestos are different naturally occurring minerals.

Talc is used in a variety of consumer and industrial products, including cosmetics and powders. It is valued for its ability to absorb moisture and provide a smooth texture.

Asbestos refers to a group of naturally occurring fibrous minerals. Exposure to asbestos is associated with serious illnesses, including mesothelioma.

The concern is that talc and asbestos may occur near one another in the earth. During mining and processing, talc may become contaminated with asbestos if the mineral deposits are not properly identified and separated.

That does not mean every talc-containing product contains asbestos.

It does mean that the source of talc, the manufacturing process, and testing practices can be important when evaluating potential exposure.

How Can Talcum Powder Become an Asbestos Exposure Concern?

Talc-containing products have been used for generations.

In cases involving alleged asbestos-contaminated talc, attorneys may investigate whether a particular product contained asbestos and whether a person’s use of that product resulted in meaningful exposure.

Questions may include which products were used, how frequently they were used, how long the use continued, and what evidence exists about the products during the relevant years.

Historical records, scientific testing, and other evidence may help establish whether asbestos was present in a particular product.

Because products and manufacturing practices can change over time, an investigation should focus on the specific circumstances rather than making assumptions about all talcum powders.

What Is the Connection Between Asbestos and Mesothelioma?

Mesothelioma is a cancer most strongly associated with asbestos exposure.

The disease may develop many years after exposure, which can make it difficult for families to identify when or where asbestos contact occurred.

A person diagnosed with mesothelioma may have worked in an industrial setting, lived with someone who worked around asbestos, or encountered asbestos through other circumstances.

In cases involving talc-containing products, the relevant issue may be whether asbestos contamination contributed to the person’s exposure history.

Medical questions about diagnosis, causation, and treatment should be discussed with qualified healthcare professionals.

What Happened in Roy v. Johnson & Johnson?

Lynne Roy was a New Orleans-area real estate professional who was diagnosed with mesothelioma in January 2020.

She died approximately four months later.

Her family’s lawsuit alleged that lifelong use of Johnson & Johnson baby powder exposed her to asbestos and contributed to her disease.

Johnson & Johnson disputed those allegations and presented evidence challenging the claimed connection between its product and Ms. Roy’s mesothelioma.

The trial also addressed other talc-containing products and potential environmental exposures.

After hearing the evidence, the Orleans Parish jury found that significant asbestos exposure associated with a Johnson & Johnson product was a cause of Ms. Roy’s mesothelioma.

The jury awarded $10 million in total damages, with approximately $2 million attributed to Johnson & Johnson and the remainder to other parties.

Why a Complete Exposure History Matters

For many families, investigating mesothelioma means looking back decades.

Potential asbestos exposure may have occurred through employment, military service, home renovations, products used in the household, or contact with materials brought home from a workplace.

Consumer product use may also be relevant in certain circumstances.

Because more than one source of exposure may exist, it is important to examine the full history rather than focusing on a single product or location.

A diagnosis does not automatically establish that a particular company or product caused someone’s illness. The available evidence and individual circumstances matter.
