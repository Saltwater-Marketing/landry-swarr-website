A $10 million jury verdict in Roy v.Johnson & Johnson has brought attention to litigation involving talc-containing baby powder, alleged asbestos exposure, and mesothelioma.

The case involved Lynne Roy, a New Orleans-area real estate professional who died from mesothelioma in 2020.

After a three-week trial in Orleans Parish, the jury found that Ms. Roy had significant exposure to asbestos in a product manufactured by Johnson & Johnson and that the exposure was a cause of her mesothelioma.

The jury awarded $10 million in total damages, holding Johnson & Johnson responsible for approximately $2 million and attributing the remainder to other parties.

Understanding that distinction is important when discussing the verdict.

What Was Roy v. Johnson & Johnson About?

Lynne Roy was diagnosed with mesothelioma in January 2020 at age 77.

Her family alleged that her lifelong use of Johnson & Johnson baby powder had exposed her to asbestos and contributed to the development of her disease.

Ms. Roy died approximately four months after her diagnosis.

The lawsuit was filed before her death and continued on behalf of her two adult daughters.

At trial, the plaintiffs presented evidence concerning her use of Johnson & Johnson baby powder and the alleged presence of asbestos in the product.

Evidence also addressed other talc-containing personal products and potential environmental exposures.

What Did Johnson & Johnson Argue?

Johnson & Johnson denied that its product caused Ms. Roy’s mesothelioma.

The company maintained that its baby powder was safe and challenged the scientific evidence offered to connect talc exposure to the disease.

The defense also argued that other potential environmental exposures should be considered.

Both sides presented testimony from witnesses in scientific and medical fields, including pathology, epidemiology, and asbestos-related disciplines.

The jury was therefore asked to evaluate competing evidence about the cause of Ms. Roy’s mesothelioma.

What Did the New Orleans Jury Decide?

Following approximately four hours of deliberation, the jury answered an important question.

It was asked whether Ms. Roy had significant exposure to asbestos in a product manufactured by Johnson & Johnson that was a cause of her mesothelioma.

The jury answered yes.

That finding was more specific than a determination that Ms. Roy had simply used talcum powder.

The question involved exposure to asbestos in a particular manufacturer’s product and whether that exposure contributed to her disease.

Why Was the $10 Million Award Divided Among Multiple Parties?

The jury also considered other potential sources of exposure.

Rather than assigning all responsibility to Johnson & Johnson, jurors allocated fault among multiple parties.

The verdict report identified Johnson & Johnson as receiving a 9% allocation of fault.

According to the attorney’s clarification of the result, Johnson & Johnson was held responsible for approximately $2 million of the total damages award, while the remaining amount was attributed to other parties.

These figures describe different aspects of the verdict. The precise legal calculation underlying Johnson & Johnson’s approximately $2 million responsibility should not be inferred simply by multiplying the total damages by the percentage of fault.

How Did the Jury Calculate $10 Million in Damages?

The jury awarded damages for the harm experienced by Ms. Roy and the losses suffered by her daughters.

The total included:

  • $3 million for physical pain and suffering
  • $3 million for mental anguish
  • $2 million for loss of enjoyment of life
  • $1 million for each daughter’s consortium claim

Together, those awards totaled $10 million.

The total verdict should not be confused with the approximately $2 million amount attributed to Johnson & Johnson.

This distinction is particularly important when reporting on cases involving multiple potentially responsible parties.

What Does the Roy Verdict Mean for Louisiana Families?

The Roy case illustrates the complexity of investigating mesothelioma.

A person’s potential exposure history may include multiple products, workplaces, or environmental circumstances.

When more than one source of asbestos exposure is alleged, determining responsibility may require detailed historical evidence and testimony.

For families facing mesothelioma, this means that identifying a single suspected exposure source may not tell the entire story.
